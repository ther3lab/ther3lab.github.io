the evolution of regulatory systems

From relatively simple prokaryotic gene-regulatory networks (GRNs), wherein transcription factors turn genes on and off, evolution has established multicellular organisms with thousands of cell types—intracellular regulation giving rise to intercellular regulation. From unwritten norms humans have concocted highly interconnected webs of regulation that span every aspect of human life. Across domains, regulatory systems increase in size and complexity as evolution proceeds. Rules beget rules. While this is qualitatively well understood, quantitatively what concrete statements can we make about the long-term evolution of regulatory systems, from simple lists of laws to highly interconnected webs of regulation? What are the Laws governing evolving Laws?

While GRN data is very useful in a cross-sectional sense—giving access to the regulatory networks of tens of thousands of organisms and associated cell types—longitudinally understanding their evolution is fraught with difficulty. The longest-running experiments, remarkably, span four decades (the “Lenski experiments”). However, this is a short period of time relative to hundreds of millions of years of evolution. Human regulatory systems do not share this limitation: there are systems for which we can see the evolution of rules from the simplest of beginnings to fine-grained laws that are highly interdependent.

In recent work, we have shown how the size and complexity of the US Code and the Laws of Cricket have increased over time and scale as power laws in effective measures of system size. Forthcoming work shows highly similar results in the regulatory frameworks of Wikipedia and in the Laws of Rugby. The Laws of Cricket are an unusually enlightening example: from around 300 years of data (the dataset precedes the American Revolutionary War) the exponential growth of the Laws in physical time is accompanied by a roughly square-root growth in the Laws with the number of matches played. This indicates that rule growth is related to the number of events encountered in the game, a relatively intuitive result, and one that echoes classical findings in the expansion of regulatory genes with genome size. Across all the datasets we have currently collected, we have also found striking similarities in their regulatory networks—power-law rule growth, power-law degree distributions, major evolutionary transitions, and increases in rule specificity. These shared features point towards universal features of regulation across social and biological domains.

Future directions will focus on developing mathematical models to explain rule growth and network evolution from mechanistic perspectives. Specifically, building on models we previously developed, we have results showing across all the rule systems studied that rule making can be seen as a response to rule bending or breaking. By considering rules as evolving in a space of behavior, the breaking of a rule (passing a threshold of behavioral deviance) causes more rules to branch (that correct for the transgression), but also increases the distance of the threshold (mimicking the response of a governor creating effective laws). This initial model motivates additional studies that will explore the evolution of regulatory networks more completely. By conducting qualitative case studies on the nature of evolution of specific rules—e.g., tax code or the laws of rugby scrums—we will identify empirically grounded mechanisms of rule evolution that can be incorporated into quantitative models. These models will then allow us to estimate the relative contributions of endogenous regulatory refinement and exogenous shocks to long-term rule growth.

The $R^3$ Lab is currently researching several questions on the evolution of regulatory systems:

  • What are the Laws governing evolving Laws?
  • Can rule making be understood, across systems as different as gene regulatory networks and legal codes, as a response to rule bending or breaking?
  • What are the relative contributions of endogenous regulatory refinement and exogenous shocks to long-term rule growth?
