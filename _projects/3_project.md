Regulatory needs are not the origin of the vast diversity and complexity that we see in biology and the world around us. Even simple dynamical systems, such as a negative feedback loop, have well characterized properties of stability. However, it is well known that both biological and social systems develop many specialized roles. Commonly, this is seen as a byproduct of the evolutionary process itself. In general, as single-celled organisms evolve, more distinct proteins are utilized; multicellular organisms evolve more cell types; ecologies acquire more species; and cities develop more occupations. As a result, regulatory architectures themselves also become highly diversified. To understand regulation as a generic phenomenon across distinct ontologies, we must also understand the ways in which systems diversify and generate novel functional elements. Therefore, one of our major goals is to develop mathematical models describing the growth of functional diversity that allow comparison of how different systems utilize and expand their diversity of functional elements.

Currently, we are constructing mathematical models that describe increasing functional diversity growth and calibrating them to data from proteomics, cities, federal agencies, and microbial ecologies. This is one of the primary goals of the $R^3$ Lab. A recent data-driven publication, and subsequent modeling-heavy follow-up study, have shown how we can map the growth of functional elements from proteomes, federal agency roles, and city occupations into a stochastic generative process that explains statistical regularities observed across all three systems. This reveals hidden statistical similarities—e.g., self-similar statistics of function abundance and rank-frequency distributions—that are shared between systems from radically different domains.

In future work, we will develop three main aspects of the framework for modeling function diversity. First, we will derive general analytic solutions for the model of functional diversity. These solutions will clarify the model's behavior across parameter space and generate additional empirical tests of its validity. Second, we are extending the range of data sources that can be explained by our unifying framework, having recently calibrated the model to microbial ecosystems which occupy a unique region of the model's parameter space. Finally, we will connect the statistical model to evolutionary models that explicitly encode the costs and benefits of functional diversity. This will involve using genetic algorithms to encode selective pressures dictating a system's evolution. Eventually, this will allow us to explain, from simple statistical approaches, how a given functional diversity or system composition is beneficial under given selective pressures.

The $R^3$ Lab is currently researching several questions on the role of diversity in complex adaptive systems:
- How does regulation as a generic phenomenon across distinct ontologies depend on the ways systems diversify and generate novel functional elements?
- Can the growth of functional elements in proteomes, federal agency roles, and city occupations be mapped onto a single, shared stochastic generative process?
- Why do microbial ecosystems occupy a unique region of the model's parameter space compared to every other complex system studied so far?
