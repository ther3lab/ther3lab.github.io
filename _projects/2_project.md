Regulatory systems evolve in large part to survive randomness induced through environmental perturbation and intrinsically stochastic function. GRNs in cells provide a great example of this: bacteria grown in different sugars will express completely different complements of proteins in order to function; exposed to heat, cells express heat-shock response proteins that activate pathways designed to keep the cell alive. Even in stable environments, gene expression is known to be highly stochastic at the level of genes, mRNAs, and proteins. Cellular dynamics have evolved to deal with this intrinsically stochastic function. While the randomness intrinsic to gene expression and the evolution of GRNs have both long been studied, much remains unknown about how regulatory systems evolve to deal with intrinsic and extrinsic noise sources.

Following past work—for which James was awarded the Reinhart-Heinrich Award in 2022 from the European Society for Mathematical and Theoretical Biology—and classic work in GRN evolution, we have been investigating the structures that arise in GRNs to deal with environmental noise, and providing a theoretical explanation for why these structures arise. A major finding is that in the face of extreme levels of environmental noise, GRNs still achieve optimal phenotypes. In addition, we found a relationship between environmental and mutational stability: robustness to environmental noise necessitates mutational stability in GRN evolution. In future work, we will investigate the explicit tradeoffs between evolvability and robustness in GRN evolution using fitness seascapes, allowing for shifting evolutionary targets. This future direction connects notions of robustness on evolutionary timescales with notions of dynamical robustness at sub-evolutionary timescales.

Finally, regulation is modeled as a steady state process rather than being an inherently time-dependent and transient process. This is often motivated by analytic tractability or "biological steady state" arguments. However, from several recent studies we have shown that considering biology to be a transient process can lead to qualitatively distinct modes of behavior that cannot occur at the steady state. These transients can exist for very long periods of time, and are not as ephemeral as "transient" might suggest—emphasizing the point that all of biology is fundamentally transient. In future work, we will focus on understanding the qualitative properties of time-dependence in biological models, where we have already discovered phase transitions in modes of behavior driven solely from the interactions between time-dependent kinetics and intrinsic system noise.

The $R^3$ Lab is currently researching several questions on randomness and heterogeneity in regulatory function:
- In the face of extreme levels of environmental noise, can GRNs still achieve optimal phenotypes—and does robustness to environmental noise necessitate mutational stability in GRN evolution?
- What are the explicit tradeoffs between evolvability and robustness in GRN evolution under a shifting fitness seascape?
- What qualitatively distinct modes of behavior can arise from treating biology as a transient, rather than steady-state, process?
